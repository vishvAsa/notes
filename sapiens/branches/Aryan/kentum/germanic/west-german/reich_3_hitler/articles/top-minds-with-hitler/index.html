Source: TW

We tend to imagine Nazi Germany as a nation of philosophical rubes, somehow stupid enough to mistake racial mythology for ethics and destiny. But Germany was arguably the most philosophically sophisticated society in Europe. This was the country of Kant and Hegel, of the neo-Kantians and the phenomenologists and the mathematicians who had just finished rebuilding the foundations of logic, and its universities were good enough that ambitious Americans and Britons still went there to get their doctorates.

Many educated Germans regarded Hitler himself as a vulgar, intellectually mediocre brute. That did not protect them. Some were terrorized into conformity; some treated him as a useful political instrument (the familiar and always mistaken idea that the crude man can be steered by the refined ones once he is in office); some compartmentalized, keeping their real thinking for the seminar room and their public conduct for everywhere else; and some supplied the regime with far more sophisticated rationalizations than Hitler could have produced himself.

Philosophy

Heidegger and Carl Schmitt are the obvious cases. Heidegger took the rectorship at Freiburg in 1933 and gave an inaugural address about the German university’s mission that was philosophically serious in a way no Party pamphlet could have been, all about knowledge, labor, and military service as three bonds of a people to its own destiny. He was already the most influential philosopher in Europe when he did it, and he did not need the job.

Schmitt had spent the previous decade developing a theory of sovereignty as the power to decide the exception, which is to say the power to declare when the normal rules do not apply, and he had worked that out on its own merits before there was a regime that wanted it. When Hitler had his own people murdered in June 1934, Schmitt published a defense of it arguing that the Führer was himself the source of law in the emergency, and two years later he organized a conference of German jurists on removing Jewish influence from German legal scholarship. Neither man was confused about what he was doing, and neither needed the regime to explain his own thoughts back to him. They arrived with the conceptual equipment already built, and the regime simply gave them an occasion to use it.

Physics

And it extended well past philosophy. Philipp Lenard won the Nobel in 1905 for cathode rays and spent the rest of his life nursing a grievance against Einstein and against theoretical physics generally, which he came to describe as a Jewish style of thinking, abstract and unmoored from honest experimental contact with nature.

Johannes Stark won the Nobel in 1919 for the effect that bears his name, and by 1924 the two of them were publishing pieces comparing Hitler to Galileo. What they built under the name Deutsche Physik was a four volume textbook and an institutional campaign to keep relativity and quantum mechanics out of German universities, run by two men who genuinely understood physics at the highest level then available. The medical case is worse, more so because of how early it starts.

Psychiatry etc.

In 1920, thirteen years before the Nazis took power, a distinguished jurist named Karl Binding and a distinguished psychiatrist named Alfred Hoche co-wrote a short book arguing for permitting the destruction of what they called life unworthy of life, and they made the argument in the ordinary register of Weimar academic humanitarianism, with attention to consent, to the suffering of the patient, to the burden on families and on the state. The Nazis did not have to invent that argument. They had to find it and staff it. Doctors went on to join the Party at a higher rate than any other profession in Germany, and it was physicians, not thugs, who designed the sterilization program and then ran the killing of disabled children and adults under a bureaucratic apparatus with a file number for an address. These were people with long training in careful thinking about hard problems.

That training did not function as an alarm.

Dissent

That said, sophistication was not anti-protective either. Kurt Huber was a professor of philosophy and musicology at Munich, a Leibniz scholar who also spent years collecting Bavarian folk songs, and politically he was a conservative German nationalist rather than anything like a leftist. He wrote the final White Rose leaflet and was executed in July 1943.

Karl Jaspers was married to Gertrud Mayer, who was Jewish, and he refused to divorce her; he was pushed out of university administration in 1933, dismissed in 1937, banned from publishing in 1938, and the two of them were on a deportation list for April 1945 when the Americans reached Heidelberg first.

Distinction

What seems to have distinguished the people who resisted was not that they had a better argument available, more so that they had prior commitments they were unwilling to renegotiate, and often that they were already outside the system of rewards and had less to lose by saying so. Alas, that is a much less flattering finding than “they thought it through and got the right answer,” because it means the relevant faculty is not the one most of us have been cultivating.

The disturbing lesson is not that philosophically naive people can believe monstrous things. It is that philosophical sophistication is not an immune system against ideology. Under sufficient institutional pressure, ambition, fear, and motivated reasoning, it can become ideology’s research-and-development department.

Historically, coercion became overwhelming after 1933 through Gleichschaltung, censorship, dismissal, imprisonment, and terror. But Nazism also had genuine popular support and drew upon older currents of völkisch nationalism, antisemitism, scientific racism, and imperial resentment. The population was neither freely unanimous nor merely forced. The more unsettling reality is that coercion, opportunism, partial belief, elite collaboration, and sincere enthusiasm reinforced one another.

Almost nobody had to accept the whole thing at once. Each step asked only for a small accommodation given what you had already accepted the month before, and the people who went along were mostly not making a decision about the endpoint, they were making a decision about the next meeting, the next signature, the next colleague they did not defend.
