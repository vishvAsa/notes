The Maharaja of Mysore constituted a committee headed by Sir M. Visvesvaraya to enquire into the disturbances, and the committee observed that law and order was completely broken down during the disturbances and the government favoured one side in the incident.[13] The members of the committee were Justice D. K. Rama Rao, H. G. Basavappa, Gulam Ahmed Kalami, V. Manickavelu Mudaliyar, B. Nagappa Bar-at-Law, and Ralph Nye.[14] The report of the committee had a "qualifying note" by one of the members, Gulam Ahmed Kalami, who did not fully agree with the findings of the committee. The committee’s report (submitted early 1929, titled Report of the Bangalore Disturbances Enquiry Committee) concluded that:

Law and order completely broke down during the disturbances.  
The government favoured one side in the incident.  
Administrative inaction, delays, and excessive centralization of power (in the Diwan’s office) contributed to the escalation.  
Police responses were inadequate or impartiality failed in places.  
There was no deep, long-standing communal conspiracy; factors included local provocations, inflammatory vernacular press, and broader tensions (e.g., related to the earlier Miller Committee reservations).

The report was critical of the administration under Diwan Mirza Ismail (who had taken office in 1926). It was described in contemporary accounts as “deeply embarrassing” to the government; Ismail’s position was seen as shaky, and public criticism (including from figures like D. V. Gundappa) focused on perceived bias and paralysis. The report was eventually published after pressure.
