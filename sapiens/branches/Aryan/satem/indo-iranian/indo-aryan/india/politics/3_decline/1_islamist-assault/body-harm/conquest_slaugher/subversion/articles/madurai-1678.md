## Rustam (or Rustum) Khan’s usurpation
After Talikota, the Madurai Nayaks (originally Vijayanagara governors) became effectively independent while still sometimes acknowledging residual Aravidu/Vijayanagara suzerainty. By the reign of Chokkanatha Nayak (r. 1659–1682), the kingdom was under pressure from Mysore, the Marathas, and internal weakness.

Rustam Khan was a Deccani Muslim cavalry officer who rose in Madurai service (under Chokkanatha or his brother/associate Muttu Alakadri). He consolidated control of the cavalry and the fort of Trichinopoly (Tiruchirappalli), then seized power around 1678–1680. He confined Chokkanatha, forced Muttu Alakadri into exile (to Negapatam), and ruled for roughly two years, treating the royal family with humiliation.

His overthrow came through a coalition of loyalists: the general Govindappaiya, Chinna Kadir Nayak, and especially **Raghunatha Kilavan** (the Sethupathi/Maravar chief of Ramnad). Accounts describe a surprise attack that killed Rustam Khan and his followers. Mysore forces were active in the region around the same time (investing Trichinopoly), and Maratha elements from Tanjore/Gingee also factored into the broader chaos, but the decisive internal strike was led by Madurai loyalists and the Maravar chief. The Nayak was restored (at least formally) until his death in 1682.

## Marathas at Kanchi/Gingee and Madurai’s stance
In the late 1680s–1690s, after Shivaji’s death and Sambhaji’s execution, Rajaram fled south and made **Gingee** (Senji) the Maratha capital-in-exile. Mughal forces under Zulfiqar Khan (Aurangzeb’s general) besieged it for years (roughly 1690–1698). Kanchipuram (Kanchi) and the surrounding Carnatic were also theatres of Maratha–Mughal conflict.

Madurai under the later Chokkanatha period and then Rani Mangammal (regent from the late 1680s) was dealing with its own survival amid Mysore raids, Maratha expansion into Tanjore, and the approaching Mughal power. Contemporary and later accounts show Madurai did **not** provide significant military help to the Marathas at Gingee. Rani Mangammal ultimately chose pragmatic accommodation with the Mughals (paying tribute) rather than open alliance against them. Tanjore Marathas did give some support to Rajaram, but Madurai’s position was more distant and cautious.

## Maravar (Ramnad) independence
The Sethupathis of Ramnad (Maravar chiefs) had long been semi-autonomous vassals or protectors under the Madurai Nayaks, tasked with guarding the Rameswaram pilgrimage route. Under Raghunatha Kilavan (late 17th century), they expanded power, shifted their capital, and asserted greater independence as Madurai weakened after Chokkanatha’s troubled reign, the Rustam Khan episode, successive wars, and the eventual collapse of the Nayak line in the 1730s.

The non-assistance to the Marathas is sometimes cited in regional narratives as one contributing grievance or symptom of Madurai’s declining authority. Ramnad effectively became an independent “Maravar kingdom” by the early 18th century.

**Summary**:
- Rustam Khan did briefly usurp power in Madurai ~1678–1680 and was overthrown with crucial help from the Maravar chief (and amid Mysore/Maratha regional activity).
- Madurai did not meaningfully aid the Marathas during the long Mughal pressure on Gingee/Kanchi.
- Maravar (Ramnad) independence grew out of Madurai’s progressive weakening rather than a single act of non-help.

The events are real and documented in sources such as V. Rangachari’s history of the Naik kingdom, Jesuit letters, and later colonial/regional histories.
