RAMAKRISHNA HEGDE  
Centenary Birthday 

Indian politics today, by and large, appears increasingly removed from the soul of the people and their aspirations. What often passes for politics is a troubling amalgam of pettiness, selfish ambition, ego, and self-deception and, at times, intellectual and moral inadequacy. The passive acquiescence of society-including sections of the educated and intellectual classes-to this distorted political culture has contributed, in my view, to a wider degeneration of moral character in our institutions and in public life.

Against this backdrop, the life and politics of Ramakrishna Hegde, whose centenary year we are celebrating stand apart.

Hegde was one of the very few political leaders of his generation who consistently believed in and practised what he called “value-based politics.” In Karnataka, he stood tall not merely because of his electoral stature, but because he brought to public life an uncompromising commitment to principle, democratic values, clean governance and human dignity. These qualities brought him national prominence, but made him a threat to those whose politics was driven primarily by ambition, factionalism and the pursuit of power.

I may be accused of being biased in Hegde’s favour. I am. I make no apology for it.

I first met him in the 1980s and remained closely associated with him until his death in 2004. I saw him not from a distance, but at close quarters-through his triumphs, disappointments, political battles, moments of doubt and moments of extraordinary courage. I saw a man who genuinely believed that public life demanded a higher standard of personal conduct. He lived by the principle that “Caesar’s wife must be above suspicion.”

His repeated resignations from public office were sometimes dismissed as his “politics of resignation.” But I often wonder: how many politicians today would voluntarily resign over allegations of phone tapping, or accept responsibility in the face of a Commission report in which they were not even directly implicated?

For Hegde, resignation was not theatre. It was an expression of accountability.

He believed that principles were not words to be invoked when convenient but disciplines by which one had to live. He believed one should never hanker after power, cling to office or indulge in dirty politics. He was deeply troubled that Indian politics had not evolved into a genuine two-party system based on clearly defined ideologies. He was frustrated by the absence of a credible national alternative to the Congress of his time and angered by opposition leaders who competed endlessly for positions, sometimes even willing to destabilise governments of which they themselves were a part. Yet Hegde remained an optimist.

Even when periodically disheartened by the conduct of politicians, he retained a deep faith that India would eventually find the political alternatives it deserved. His belief in the possibility of a better India was never extinguished by temporary political failure.

## A Different Idea of Nationalism

Hegde was, above all, a nationalist-but his nationalism was neither narrow nor sectarian. For him, nationalism meant human dignity, social integration, democratic principles and decentralisation of power. It did not mean the fragmentation of society into competing vote banks based on religion, caste, community or region. He was deeply concerned about the erosion of national identity in the name of protecting sectional identities. He warned:

> “We are now promoting diversities in the name of protecting group identities at the cost of national identity and unity. Policy formulation has today been reduced to catering to the vote banks of sectional interests which often go against the national interest. In the process, we have lost national ambition, national character, national pride and national will. The spectre of national disintegration is staring at our face today.”

Those words, spoken decades ago, seem remarkably prescient today.

The politics of narrow sectional interests, the weakening of principles in public life and political alliances formed primarily for access to power rather than shared ideology would have deeply disturbed him. Hegde believed that political life required a larger national purpose.

## The Making of a National Alternative

I remember vividly how, in 1987-88, Hegde deliberately put aside his own political interests and political future to pursue something larger: the creation of a credible national alternative to the Congress. He was convinced that coalition politics was the way forward. He believed that like-minded political forces had to come together if India was to have a viable alternative at the Centre.

When the controversies surrounding Bofors, Quattrocchi and the HDW submarine deal began to dominate public discourse, Hegde approached V. P. Singh, then Defence Minister in Rajiv Gandhi’s Cabinet.

V. P. Singh had emerged as the anti-corruption face of Indian politics, but he was initially hesitant to leave the Congress and uncertain about his ability to lead a national alternative. At the many meetings held at our residence in Delhi, he was often diffident and urged Hegde to speak to the Opposition leaders and build consensus around his leadership. It was Hegde who undertook that enormous task.

He reached out to Devi Lal, Chandrashekhar, Madhu Dandavate, Biju Patnaik, I. K. Gujral, Lalu Prasad Yadav, George Fernandes and several others. He also had to bring together leaders from Karnataka, including S. R. Bommai and H. D. Deve Gowda. He worked tirelessly, almost with missionary zeal, to persuade these disparate leaders to come together and form the Janata Dal. The massive rally at the Bangalore Palace Grounds on 11 October 1988 became a defining moment. The formation of the Janata Dal generated enormous hope. The media understandably focused on V. P. Singh, who became the rallying point of the new formation. But I witnessed what happened behind the scenes. It was Hegde who painstakingly knitted together this formidable collection of political personalities.

He worked towards the creation of the National Front Government without seeking a ministerial position for himself. V. P. Singh offered him the Deputy Chairmanship of the Planning Commission, which Hegde accepted with characteristic grace. When I repeatedly asked him why he was not in the Cabinet, his answer was simple. There were enough “big” leaders seeking positions. For him, the success of the National Front coalition-and its ability to complete its full term-would be a greater achievement than holding a ministerial office. That was Hegde. He could distinguish between power and purpose.

When Power Overwhelmed Principle

Unfortunately, the coalition soon revealed the vulnerabilities inherent in Indian politics. The struggle was no longer merely ideological. Ego, insecurity and the desire to retain power began to undermine the very coalition that had been created as a national alternative. When Deputy Prime Minister Devi Lal announced a major farmers’ rally in Haryana, V. P. Singh, fearing that his own position was threatened, hurriedly organised another rally and announced acceptance of the Mandal Commission recommendations without adequately consulting his Cabinet or the senior leaders supporting his government. Hegde understood what this meant.

He knew the Government was becoming unstable. When the BJP withdrew support, the National Front Government eventually collapsed. Chandrashekhar became Prime Minister with outside support from the Congress. For Hegde, this was profoundly disappointing. The very purpose of the Janata Dal and the National Front had been to create a credible national opposition to the Congress. The experiment had failed, not because the idea was wrong, but because political ambition proved stronger than political principle.

## A Ringside View of Indian Politics

I often say that during those years I had a ringside view of Indian politics. Prime Ministers came into our home. I listened to conversations about politics, power, insecurity, personal prejudices and national affairs. I saw these extraordinarily powerful people not merely as Prime Ministers but as human beings.

Chandrashekhar and I. K. Gujral enjoyed my presence when they met Hegde. They often told me that I brought a fresh perspective and a certain joie de vivre that lightened the atmosphere. Perhaps what amused them was that I was entirely nonchalant about their enormous political power. I had never sought anything from them or from their governments. With V. P. Singh and Atal Bihari Vajpayee, I remained respectful and formal. Both attended my performances and were unfailingly courteous and appreciative. My relationship with H. D. Deve Gowda was, of course, altogether different.

## Hegde and Deve Gowda

The tumultuous relationship between Ramakrishna Hegde and H. D. Deve Gowda is now part of political folklore. But very few people know what actually happened behind the scenes. After the collapse of the National Front, the political formation fragmented. Yet in Karnataka, the Janata Dal remained a significant force largely because of Hegde’s immense personal popularity. Deve Gowda and S. R. Bommai recognised that they would be stronger going into the 1994 Assembly elections with Hegde as their leader. Deve Gowda therefore visited Hegde at his Bangalore residence, Krithika, and expressed his willingness to work under his leadership. Hegde, being the open-hearted and decent man that he was, accepted those words at face value. He threw himself into preparations for the elections.

The Janata Dal won decisively in Karnataka, emerging as the single largest party with 115 seats. A large proportion of these victories came from North Karnataka and other regions where Hegde enjoyed enormous popularity. On the evening of the results, Hegde and Biju Patnaik, who had come as the central observer, went to Vidhana Soudha. What they encountered was astonishing. Hundreds of people had been brought in by Deve Gowda’s supporters & son H.D Revanna to demand his appointment as Chief Minister. They shouted slogans and heckled Hegde. 

Hegde was stunned. He had regarded the victory as a collective achievement. Before anyone could intervene, he simply said that he was not in the race for Chief Ministership and walked away. He came directly with Biju Patnaik to Ravindra Kalakshetra, where I was performing. After my performance, they came home for a quiet meal and conversation. Many legislators from North Karnataka, Central Karnataka and Bangalore were uncertain about what to do and were looking towards Hegde for direction. Hegde could easily have split the party and formed a government with outside support. He refused. He believed that government formation should be smooth and that his role should be that of an integrator, never a disruptor.

I remember being deeply upset at the manner in which he had been treated. Biju Patnaik, however, praised Hegde’s conduct as mature and statesmanlike. That moment revealed the essential difference between Hegde and many politicians: he was willing to lose office rather than compromise his understanding of democratic propriety.

## The United Front and Another Lost Opportunity

The Janata Dal Government was eventually formed under H. D. Deve Gowda. Soon afterwards, the country went into another general election. Hegde once again campaigned extensively for Janata Dal candidates across Karnataka, Bihar, Orissa, Kerala, the North-East and elsewhere. The result was a hung Parliament. Atal Bihari Vajpayee formed the Government but it lasted only 13 days.

The subsequent effort to create the United Front brought Hegde once again to the centre of the political negotiations. Jyoti Basu was widely regarded as a possible Prime Ministerial candidate. But he faced opposition within his own party and was reluctant to lead a coalition comprising parties with widely divergent ideologies.

Many leaders believed Hegde would be more capable of bringing them together. His name began circulating. Once again, Hegde did not aggressively seek the position. He said he would accept a consensus candidate. The leaders were invited to Bihar Bhavan to decide upon the next Prime Minister. But politics took another turn.

Lalu Prasad Yadav and several others did not initially appear. When they eventually arrived, the decision was announced: H. D. Deve Gowda would be the Prime Ministerial candidate. The meeting ended. Hegde and I. K. Gujral came to our flat on Ferozeshah Road. Gujral teasingly said that the next Prime Minister was from my State. I was delighted. But life had another plan.

There were more phone calls, more conversations and more explanations. V. P. Singh told us that Deve Gowda had visited him with 16 Karnataka MPs and represented himself as the undisputed leader of Karnataka, while suggesting that Hegde was not interested in the Prime Ministership.

The Karnataka MPs subsequently arrived at our house. They acknowledged having accompanied Deve Gowda but claimed they had not fully understood what had been discussed. It was yet another extraordinary episode in a political world where words, loyalties and realities often seemed to shift by the hour.

## The Hegde Way

On the eve of Deve Gowda’s swearing-in as the 11th Prime Minister of India, S. R. Bommai visited our home. He appeared apologetic and confused and suggested that he and Hegde should withdraw from public life. I challenged him. I asked why he was advising Hegde to abandon politics when he himself was about to enter the Union Cabinet. Hegde returned to Karnataka nevertheless. He suggested J. H. Patel as Chief Minister, a choice accepted by most legislators.

When J. H. Patel later publicly suggested that he was not Chief Minister because of Hegde, a large group of his Ministers gathered at my home and offered to resign. Hegde refused to allow them to do so. Once again, he insisted that the Government should remain stable and complete its term. He would not permit anyone to demonstrate personal loyalty to him by resigning. He did this repeatedly-whether under Bommai, Deve Gowda or J. H. Patel.

His political instinct was always towards stability, accommodation and institutional continuity, rather than revenge.

## Expelled from His Own Party

Then came what was perhaps the deepest political wound of Hegde’s life. On 13 June 1996, Janata Dal President Lalu Prasad Yadav expelled Ramakrishna Hegde from the party. No show-cause notice. No charges. No formal process. Simply an expulsion order released to the press. The irony was devastating.

This was the party Hegde himself had helped build, brick by brick, through enormous effort and sacrifice. His decency, generosity, accommodative nature and refusal to indulge in aggression had not protected him. In the end, he had been expelled from the very political formation he had helped create. Hegde was deeply shaken.

I, along with many of his followers, urged him to establish another political platform. He initially resisted the idea of forming a political party and instead created the Rashtriya Nava Nirmana Vedike, a non-political organisation. I became its National General Secretary, with Hegde as its President. Eventually, after months of persuasion and discussion, Hegde formed Lok Shakti in February 1997.

Several other political organisations subsequently merged their identities with Lok Shakti, giving it a national character.

## The Vajpayee Years

By this time, Indian politics was changing again. Atal Bihari Vajpayee had begun speaking regularly with Hegde. Vajpayee understood that the BJP needed credible, secular and progressive political leaders if it was to gain wider public acceptance. Hegde eventually concluded that Vajpayee would be a far better Prime Minister than many of the alternatives available at the time.

Thus began another political experiment: Hegde and Lok Shakti joining the NDA.

Leaders such as Surjit Singh Barnala, Thambidurai, Maneka Gandhi, George Fernandes, Ram Jethmalani, Nitish Kumar and others regularly met at Hegde’s official residence at 83, Lodi Estate, where he served as Commerce Minister. Hegde became an important bridge between different political constituencies. Yet, in 1999, Vajpayee dropped him, after explaining to him at length that George Fernandes wanted him to.

It was a blow from which Hegde never fully recovered. I remain convinced that the NDA could not have come to power in 1998 without the support of Hegde and other leaders of impeccable secular and progressive credentials who helped give the alliance greater political acceptability. Hegde’s subsequent decision to merge Lok Shakti with Janata Dal (United) in 1999 was, in my view, a political mistake. I had warned him against it. Yet his deep emotional attachment to the Janata Dal tradition remained stronger than his political caution.

## The Price of Standing by Someone

Public life has an unforgiving rule. When a leader succeeds, everyone around him claims credit. When he fails, those around him become the convenient targets. I became one of those targets. There were many who accused me of influencing Hegde’s decisions and even of being responsible for his political setbacks, particularly his decision to join the NDA.

But I continue to ask: given the political circumstances of 1998 and 1999, was there a better Prime Ministerial choice than Atal Bihari Vajpayee? Lok Shakti won three Parliamentary seats. Hegde returned to national relevance and became Union Commerce Minister. Given the alternatives before the country at that moment, I continue to believe that his decision was defensible.

More importantly, Hegde himself never blamed me. On the contrary, he repeatedly told people that I had been his strength-that my steadfastness and support had helped him survive the many political blows that came his way.

## Beyond Politics

There was another Hegde whom the public rarely saw. The birth of our sons, Chirantan and Chirayu, brought him immense happiness at a time when his political life was going through extraordinary turbulence. We travelled together. We played golf. We attended performances and plays. We listened to music. We shared meals, conversations and laughter in the privacy of our home. Those were moments when politics receded and the human being emerged. And that human being was warm, affectionate, humorous, generous and deeply capable of friendship.

## A Man Who Stood Apart

Ramakrishna Hegde was, to me, a complete human being. He was clear-headed and farsighted. He possessed a high standard of personal character, an extraordinary sense of public duty and an unwavering commitment to democratic principles. He believed that politics must have a moral foundation. He believed that power was a means, not an end.He believed that government existed to serve people, not political ambition.

He believed in national unity without demanding uniformity; in democracy without reducing it to numbers; in decentralisation without weakening national purpose; and in political alternatives without sacrificing principles.

Above all, he believed that public life demanded personal integrity.

His political journey was turbulent. He made mistakes. He suffered betrayals. He experienced political defeat, humiliation and exclusion. Yet through all of it, he rarely surrendered the essential decency that defined him. He chose principle over expediency. He chose reconciliation over revenge. He chose Governmental stability over personal power. Again and again, he placed the larger political and national purpose before his own ambition.

That is why, when I look at Indian public life today, I feel the absence of men like Ramakrishna Hegde so profoundly. He was not perfect. No human being is. But he possessed something increasingly rare in public life: the ability to hold power without being possessed by it.

Ramakrishna Hegde lived a full and consequential life. He was a nationalist without being sectarian, a democrat without being populist, a politician without being consumed by power, and a statesman who understood that the credibility of public institutions ultimately rests upon the character of those who occupy them.

The likes of him, I fear, we may never see again.”

PRATHIBHA PRAHLAD
