## Sadāśiva's Rājaratnākara
Sadāśiva's Rājaratnākara (comp. 1677), a poetic history of Rāṇā Rāj Singh I (24 September 1629 – 22 October 1680) of Mevāṛ on the contemporary perception of Muḥammad Dārā Shikōh (20 March 1615 – 30 August 1659) recalling the Mughal War of Succession:


> This eldest son, Dara Shikoh, is featured in the next five verses of Rāja-ratnākara, providing us with an alternative perspective on a controversial figure who is often presented today as Aurangzeb’s alter ego.⁶⁸ While Raj Singh was engaged in attacking neighbouring towns, an envoy from Dara Shikoh arrived with a letter. In it, the Mughal prince made this plea:
>
> Those three brothers of mine,
They long to suppress Hindu practices and scriptures.  
Master of the Hindus, whose mind is enveloped in dharma!  
How could you have affection for these wicked ones?⁶⁹
>
> While my father Shah Jahan is alive and
I, the eldest son, remain favourably disposed to you,  
For you, who is steadfast in his own dharma and loves god,  
To cross over boundaries, as does the sea, is not proper.⁷⁰
>
> Therefore, Raja, understand as truth these words
of mine and to your own capital  
Go back! I will feel friendship toward you for as long  
as I live, never letting go of our affection.⁷¹
>
> Rāja-ratnākara’s poet has here put an overtly religious appeal in Dara Shikoh’s mouth, calling on Hindu rulers to support his claim to the throne because to do otherwise would be harmful to their faith. His brothers, unlike Dara Shikoh himself, are allegedly intent on suppressing Hindu ways. The poem shows us that, less than twenty years after his death, Dara Shikoh was remembered by at least some Hindus as more favourably inclined toward their “practices and scriptures” (dharmāgama) than Aurangzeb and his other brothers.


## Rāj-vilās
Another biography of Rāṇā Rāj Singh I of Mevāṛ, the Rāj-vilās (comp. 1677-1680[?]) by Mān Kavi, frames the Mevāṛi participation in the War of the Succession of Mārvāṛ (23 July 1679 – 12 March 1707) in explicitly religious terms and with a recourse to Mevāṛi history:


> Primeval is the Hindu-Muslim [asura] enmity,  
both dharmas have a use in the world.  
In the millions of eons that have elapsed,  
mightily have they fought (repeatedly).³⁹
>
> Because Hindus are the world’s foundation,  
the Hindu king is the greatest on earth.  
Just as the husband (lords it) over the wife,  
so too the Hindu ruler heads the world.⁴⁰
>
> When mlecchas become powerful on earth,  
Vishnu knows it immediately.  
He himself took 10 incarnations,  
to bring about the demons’ end.⁴¹
>
> Rana Raj Singh,  
just as did Vishnu’s incarnations on earth,  
Vowed to win in battle,  
against Aurang who was like a demon-lord.⁴²
>
> Here Rana Raj Singh stands as a symbol of the eternal Hindu struggle against demons, a category in which the Mughal emperor is explicitly included. A clear opposition between the Mewari hero of the poem and the current Mughal ruler is thus articulated at the very outset of Canto 9. This type of explicit invocation of religious strife or competition never figures in the cantos (10–18) on the actual fighting in Mewar, in a noteworthy contrast. Canto 9 next mention a few aspects of Aurangzeb’s career.⁴³


> Next, the poet turns briefly to the issue of religious difference, in even more pointed terms than at the beginning of Canto 9, when Mewar’s Rana Raj Singh was equated to lord Vishnu and the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb to one of Vishnu’s demon opponents. After several verses on the incompatibility and distrust between Marwar’s Maharaja Jasvant Singh and the Shah Aurang as individuals, the poet notes that their basic nature and religious practices are antithetical: one is a Ksatriya, the other is a Turk; one follows the Vedic injunctions, the other observes the way of the book; one’s mouth recites “Rama”, the other’s tongue repeats (the name of) the Prophet; one honours the eastern direction, the other bows in the western direction, and the like. This seems to suggest that Jasvant Singh and Aurangzeb could not possibly have gotten along given their differing faiths, and also implies more broadly that Hindus and Muslims were fundamentally at odds.


> The poem’s narrative gaze now returns to its main hero, Rana Raj Singh, who is once again praised as the lord of Hindus (hindūpati), maintainer of kṣatriya dharma, a world-renowned great king (puhavī prasiddha chatrapati), and the like. 
> 
> The Majestic Rana and his illustrious Sisodiya dynasty are compared favourably to the villainous (pisuna) emperor of demons (asura), Aurangzeb. The Rana agrees to give refuge to Jodhpur’s Ajit Singh, at least partly on the grounds of the marital ties (saggapan) between the two royal families, and provides him with 12 villages for his sustenance (Rāj-vilās 9.171–205). Then, the Rana proclaims to the Rathors of Jodhpur:
>
> I spread the superior Veda and will preserve on earth the Puranas,  
The qazi’s books and all the Qurans, I reduce all these to ashes,  
I will grind down the Chagatai⁵⁵ and establish my own garrison in Delhi,  
I maintain Hindu customs and uproot the demonic (āsurī) ways,  
I will raise up the best holy temples⁵⁶ and tear down the mleccha sites,  
I will protect all the Rathors, the angry Rana Raj said.⁵⁷

> A second conspicuous aspect of Rāj-vilās’ narrative of war is its recourse to history. When considering what strategy to take in dealing with the Mughal troops occupying their territory, the elite warriors of Mewar are counselled to consider how Mewar kings had successfully resisted the Mughals for decades, from the late 1560s to 1615. The lesson to be learned from the past was that the Mewar warriors should retreat to the hills, defend the passes, and harass the Mughal forces with raids and ambushes. This practical advice also created a sense of continuity with the past, a past in which Mewar was at war with the empire, just as it was at the current moment. Rāj-vilās thus posits resistance to the Mughals as a central element in Mewar identity through the ages. 
> 
> History is again employed as a means of understanding the present in Rāj-vilās’ account of the causes of the Rajput Rebellion of 1679–81. Aurangzeb’s policy toward Marwar after Jasvant Singh’s death is traced back to his animus for the Maharaja from the time of the War of Succession, in 1658–59. Conveniently omitting mention of the many years of military service Jasvant Singh provided to the empire in the Deccan and elsewhere, as well as his very high rank at court,⁵⁸ Rāj-vilās casts the Marwar Maharaja as having always been defiant of Mughal authority. By implication, Maharaja Jasvant Singh is therefore comparable to the celebrated rulers of Mewar who also fought against the imperial overlords. Through his allusion to past hostilities, Rāj-vilās’ poet thus cleverly insinuates that a state of conflict with the Mughal empire was a normal state of affairs for the Rajputs of Mewar and Marwar.
