De-Islamization of Georgia’s Adjara Province: A Case Study

Krishna S. Yunjan 

May 24, 2026



The country of Sakartvelo \(more well-known by its exonym Georgia\) is the heartland of the southern Caucasus, between the Black and the Caspian Seas. Its peoples, the Kartvelians, have inhabited this area for at least 8000 years. 



## Religious and National Character



From Mithraism and other traditional religions, they were eventually converted to Eastern Orthodox Christianity in 337 CE under King Mirian III. The Christianization of Sakartvelo happened shortly after its southern neighbor, Armenia, also adopted Christianity following a few episodes of violent internal conflict - including against a vibrant Hindu community that once thrived there and had opposed Christian aggression. 



The Armenian language and genetics is classified as an Indo-European variant, whereas Kartvelians speak a language considered an isolate. Kartvelians are also genetically distinct from Indo-Europeans, with far more in common with West Asians. 



In their interactions with neighboring empires - such as the Ottoman Turks, Safavid Iranians, and Tsarist and Soviet Russians - the Georgians consistently achieved prominence within the military and administrative elites of those empires, whereas Armenians typically were a trading, merchant community, peddling influence via their mercantile networks, often in league with Jewish networks. Georgia also has one of the oldest Jewish diaspora communities, which didn’t experience any persecution in 2600 years. This is something most Israeli Jews know about. Indians often make the false claim that they are the ‘only’ country that never persecuted Jews, a notion indulged by Israeli counterparts.



## Sacred Geography



Kartvelian civilization thrived along the River Mtkvari, also known as the Kura. This river originates in what is today north-eastern Turkiye, flows the length of Sakartvelo, enters what is today Azerbaijan, and ends in the Caspian Sea.



North-eastern Turkiye, in which the River Mtkvari originates, is historically Kartvelian land. These comprise the provinces of Artvin, Rize, and Ardahan, with heavy cultural influence also extending into parts of Erzurum and Kars. The people there had been Islamized and Turkified during and after the Ottoman invasion and occupation of large parts of Western Sakartvelo, using both coercion and incentivization. Similarly, the Azeris to the east are late entrants to the region.



## Christian Cultural Nationalism in Georgia: Geopolitical Background



The Turkic Islamization of Christian Georgia also included the Black Sea province of Adjara, which is now within the borders of post-Soviet Sakartvelo. At the time of independence after the collapse of the USSR, the population of this province was almost 100% Muslim. Today, this is estimated to be at 30% to 40% and falling. This is after a massive, systematic, government-backed effort by the Georgian Orthodox Church to bring their Islamized brothers ‘back home’.



The re-Christianization drive of Georgian Muslims was pioneered by His Holiness Ilia II, the long-serving Patriarch of the Georgian Orthodox Church for nearly five decades, who recently passed away on March 17, 2026, at the age of 93. He is widely popular and also oversaw the strengthening of Christian nationalism at the grassroots level.



In the post-Soviet era, this region has seen major upheaval. Georgia swung towards Westernization, and the Russians invaded and still occupy the northern provinces of Abkhazia and South Ossetia. Yet, Georgia has so far continued to nurture a delicate but stable democratic system in spite of periodic protests, balancing its Western ties with a gradual re-engagement with Russia. 



On the other hand, Azerbaijan has benefitted immensely from exporting its natural gas resources, and is riding a wave of prosperity, but lacks democratic institutions, with a strict top-down authoritarian form of government. It has engineered major population displacements to facilitate resource drilling or geopolitical objectives, and cracked down on protests using draconian measures.



Armenia is apparently the sick man of the Caucasus at this point, facing existential pressure from the Turkic pincer of Turkiye in the west and Azerbaijan in the east, in spite of aligning itself with the United States - in which it has a significant and successful diaspora - and also maintaining friendly relations with its southern neighbor, Iran. 

Clearly, neither has the wealthy and educated Armenian diaspora in the United States been able to influence policy there beyond token gestures such as the bare acknowledgment of the Armenian Genocide \(1915-1923\) under the Ottomans, nor has its appeal to past and present human rights emergencies elicited much condemnation from its Western allies.



In this milieu, only Kartvelian Christian nationalist Georgia has managed to maintain reasonably friendly relations with all countries in the region, as well as reasonable domestic economic development - all while conducting a long-term Christianization campaign of its Islamic minorities. It is significant that this has been accomplished while maintaining neutral relations with neighboring Azerbaijan and Turkiye. 



It is also to be noted that foreign Islamist entities have simultaneously attempted to radicalize Georgian Muslims, with limited success. Therefore, it is noteworthy how Georgia managed to re-convert its Islamized minority to Christianity while balancing international relations with neighboring Islamist countries and also handling Islamic radicalism on the home front.



## Re-Christianization Strategies in Georgia



It is worth mentioning that the foundations of post-Soviet Georgian Christian nationalism were laid in the final decade of the USSR itself. During the Perestroika period of internal liberal reform, Georgian cultural nationalists decided to make Christianity the cornerstone of Georgian identity - ignoring pre-Christian traditions and history, as well as modern era spiritual and cultural trends.



As an example, a national architectural monument called the Chronicles of Georgia was commissioned in 1981, and was executed by the Kartvelian sculptor Zurab Tsereteli upon a hill overlooking the capital Tbilisi. Today it is advertised as a prominent and iconic tourist and ethnographic attraction. In it, the very identity of being Georgian is tied to being Christian, implying that the Kartvelians were not as coherent a nation during pre-Christian times. This latter implication is quite debatable.





The national Kartuli language and script have also been crafted to render repeated, explicit associations to Christian heritage, and this is touted as an example of Georgia’s fundamental Christianness in schools. Biblical phrases and idioms are preferred in all forms of communication, and so are choices of personal and place names.



History is taught mostly in terms of figures marked for their defence of, both, the land as well as of Christianity. The Modi administration in India had used this Christian nationalism to cement its relationship with Sakartvelo, when EAM Jaishankar returned the remains of Queen Ketevan to Georgia in 2021. She was abducted by Shah Abbas of Iran, and strangled to death upon her refusal to accept Islam. A Georgian monk smuggled her mortal remains to Hindu India, where it has been safely kept for centuries.



In history textbooks, pre-Christian Kartvelian religion is described as nonsensical and ignorant paganism, a claim average Kartvelians have completely absorbed, with few able to provide much detail about it.



One of the most visible movements of re-Christianization has been the significant government investment in the building of churches across neighborhoods in all towns, notable for their traditional aesthetics and architectural solidity. The architectural style is archaic, although the structures are modern. Similarly, any hill or prominent point in even smaller towns will have a chapel or at least a crucifix planted atop.



## De-Islamization Strategies in Georgia



While the Spanish Reconquista is frequently cited as an example of de-Islamization of a previously colonized territory, a very recent and continuing example is the Adjara province of Sakartvelo \(Georgia\), as mentioned in the foregoing section. The Georgian Orthodox Church in league with the US-aligned Georgian government has not only promoted Christian nationalism, but also a targeted strategy of mass conversion of the Georgian Muslim minority back to Christianity.



Adjaria or Adjara fell under the Ottoman Caliphate for three centuries, and a great deal of pressure and inducement were brought upon the inhabitants to accept Islam. Now fully Islamized, many Adjarian Muslims still learn Turkish as a second language.



As part of the Soviet Union, Communist indoctrination helped loosen the mental colonization of Islam on the minds of Adjarian Muslims. In other ex-Soviet states with Muslim heritage, a cycle of re-Islamization occurred, often accompanied by some violence in the attempt to take over the government. However, in Adjaria, this was not the case, due to it falling within the borders of a Christian-majority nation of Sakartvelo \(Georgia\).



The Kartvelian state actively engaged Adjarian Muslims, and re-affirmed their kinship with them as ethnic Kartvelians by language, genetics, and culture - sans Turkification. In doing so, the Christian Georgians did not make any effort to assimilate Turkic or Islamic cultural or linguistic elements into their mainstream culture, any more than it already existed. They simply affirmed that the kinship rested on elements that were purely Kartvelian and nothing else.



The social discourse and all educational material in Georgia emphasized that Islam was an invading force that patriotic Kartvelians fought and sacrificed against. They also emphasized that those Kartvelians who converted to Islam did so under great duress or inducement, with Sufi *tariqats* playing the contrasting agent.



The Georgian state apparatus also protected those who critique Turkish and Islamic propaganda, social practices, and institutions such as foreign-funded *madrassas* promoting radical views, calls for imposing greater *hijab*-compliance on women, or the calumny of other religions among local Muslims. Islamic religious, social or historical propaganda was not allowed to go uncontested even within Muslim-majority areas like Adjara. Those who spoke up were kept safe from threats, overt or implied. Those who made threats were dealt with swiftly.+++(5)+++



Adjaria also has its own unique historical Kartvelian style of mosques. However, far from advertising these on the tourism circuit, or even preserving these as part of national heritage, the Georgian state let Adjaria’s historic mosques fall into disrepair. This is a reciprocal policy for non-Islamic places of worship within Islamic states - temples or churches are either converted to mosques, or allowed to fall into ruin, with no renovations or repairs allowed. Some media coverage on the European Left had criticized this policy of Georgia and tried to highlight Georgia’s “diversity” and unique Muslim culture, but none of this was entertained by the Georgian state or tourism industry, which is 20% of its economy \(including tourism-dependent sections of the economy\). 

In fact, Adjara’s tourist hub is the Black Sea resort of Batumi, which has a more Europeanized, Westernized and modern feel than the capital Tbilisi, with government-built churches being the only visible traditional element in the prominent parts of the cityscape. 

Just as in the rest of the country, the state built churches throughout Adjaria, which was a 100% Muslim province until the end of the Soviet era.

### Pork

Georgia also continued a policy of mainstreaming pork at all grocery stores, including in Adjara. For centuries, Georgia’s Christian society had integrated pork into its agriculture, culinary traditions, and folklore, unlike neighboring Islamic societies. The modern Kartvelian state continues this policy without any sensitivity to Muslim “sensibilities”.

### Wine

Ancient Sakartvelo is also considered the world’s first wine-making culture in traditional open qvevri fermentation vessels. This was likely part of the Mithraic religious culture. A center of Mithraism and wine-making was Adjara. This aspect of Adjaran heritage is highlighted by the social discourse, rather than any aspect of Islamic heritage. Wine is prohibited by Islamic law.

## Singing

Another contribution of Kartvelian heritage to world culture is polyphonic singing. Different regions within Georgia have their own signature version of polyphonic folk singing, and Adjaran polyphonic songs are the most complex. This local culture and talent is nurtured by the Kartvelian state, along with traditional Georgian folk dance which includes males and females together. This form of local engagement is exclusively on the basis of shared culture that is purely Kartvelian. More often than not, such engagement goes against the grain of Islamic culture.



## The Reaction of Georgian Muslims to Christian Nationalism



Through the period of Re-Christianization and De-Islamization, Adjara has also seen the emergence of radical Islam, just like in other parts of the Caucasus. Foreign-funded *madrassas*, sudden ubiquity of *hijab*, radical political views, learning Turkish and Arabic languages and literature, and joining Islamic terrorist organizations such as ISIS - these are some of the symptoms of the simultaneous rise of Islamic radicalism within Georgia.



However, as mentioned above, prominent national voices in Sakartvelo constantly criticized these trends with the full protection of the state. It is fair to say that a large section of educated Kartvelian Muslims preferred a lifestyle based on progressive values. The constant refrain of Georgia’s media and social discourse was to identify Islam with regressive values and Christianity as a progressive force. This resonated with many, who converted as a way of being a part of the mainstream. 



At all times, conversion out of Islam into Christianity was not allowed to endanger the security of converts \(since apostasy is punishable by death and the murderer is rewarded in the afterlife, according to Islamic theology\).+++(5)+++



In this manner, by engaging large sections of Kartvelian Muslims intellectually, culturally, and emotionally in a safe space, mass conversions were effected. Those who did not convert would inevitably be marginalized.



## Chavchavadze’s Choice: Georgian Liberals and Christian Ethno-Nationalism



The current ruling party, Georgian Dream, is an ultra-right wing Orthodox Christian nationalist party. This ideology has been dominant for most of the post-Soviet era. However, liberal values have a strong foothold in modern Kartvelian ethos: The intellectual father of modern Kartvelian nationhood, Ilia Chavchavadze, asserted that religion should not have anything to do with ethnicity or nationhood. He laid out these thoughts as far back as his writings between 1877 to 1881. Harking back to that era, Kartvelian liberals today oppose religious ethno-nationalism. 



Georgian liberals also accuse the ethno-nationalists of deliberately impeding Georgia’s integration with Europe \(which has been extremely liberal-progressive until recently\) due to this Christian nationalism. This argument may, however, expire soon, for it appears that Georgian-style Christian ethno-nationalism might be ahead of the curve leading the rest of Europe.



The major part of the reconversion drive was during the 1990s up until 2010, during which it was mostly unimpeded. Since then, however, Kartvelian liberals have raised their voices in defense of the remaining Muslim minority in Upper Adjara. They routinely stand with Muslims in their fight against local municipalities that block the construction of new mosques, restrict Islamic schooling, or raise “Islamophobic” concerns during local elections.



One reason it is doubtful whether this politics of liberals will get any traction among most Kartvelians is the fundamental incompatibility of liberal values and the Islamic values they are standing up for against the more compatible Western Christian values. It appears hypocritical to average Georgians.



Another reason the liberal ideal of nationhood is anachronistic is that the views of Chavchavadze outlined above were during a phase where Kartvelians had lost sovereignty and were under the Russian Empire. In those circumstances, it is psychologically, politically, and socially more feasible to albeit temporarily remove religion from the social equation and create a sense of national unity based on shared ethnic traits alone. This may be compared to Subhash Chandra Bose’s or the Congress Party’s variety of Indian nationalism under the British Empire. That Nehruvian ideologues took it further within a sovereign India even after that policy had failed catastrophically in a bloody Partition is a different matter.



In fact, a present day look at North Caucasus under Russian rule reveals a more extreme form of the same phenomenon: Tribes in Trans-Caucasia that are divided into Christian and Muslim adherents are drastically loosening those religious bonds in order to come together more tightly under the umbrella of pre-Islamic and pre-Christian pagan traditions. Pagan revivalism turns Christo-Islamic religious war into immediate brotherhood.



Modern Africa has also become a bloody battleground between co-ethnic Africans who are now divided into Christian and Muslim communities. An increasing number of intellectuals and activists for African unity are harking back to African Traditional Religion as the only solution to intractable religious strife and to bring back brotherhood.



Thus, Chavchavadze’s choice is more relevant during times of loss of sovereignty and the need for national unity in a situation of a more or less evenly divided religious demographic. However, when a nation has agency, it can be used to effect a more wholesome and meaningful unity that includes a shared spiritual culture. 

That spiritual culture differs from one civilization to another. In the case of Japanese nationhood, there is a great freedom of religion, but a fundamental shared respect for Shintoism as the national soul. 

Indian nationalism is very similar in that it is a two-tier cultural ecology with a meta-religious, meta-linguistic and meta-cultural substrate upon which **fractal localism** flourishes.
