Slavery

Until the end of WW1 many of the “Tribal Areas” of Assam, the Naga Hills and Upper Burma was terra-incognita for the Brits because they were satisfied with leaving them to their “barbarity”, when they did they found a thriving slave-trade based around debt-bondage and raiding.

Notices of human-sacrifice in Naga-Kachin country in Upper Burma.

Incidences actually increased after the 1890s as increasing populations and depleting fertility of the farmland incentivised rites to propitiate the Nats (supernatural spirits/essences) with souls for benefits.

Human sacrifice

Some of these involved slaves being left to die in the fields during controlled burnings of shifting-cultivation, others were subject to foundation-sacrifices where the slave was crushed under the posts of the house, the most common kind however was when a Shaman prescribed it:

It was severe enough that we know of slave-raids for human-sacrifices into Assam where several Indian workers and labourers were taken:

As late as the mid-1920s, Naga chiefs were obstinate to the point where British officers could get them to give up slavery if compensated, but not human-sacrifices for the spirits, without considering the possibility of an actual expedition to suppress the practice.

Transcribed text from the three images in the quoted tweet (combined in reading order):

The Nagas appear to be peculiarly degraded savages … On the question of human sacrifice I found them obstinate and even fanatical. The practice, they said, was part of their faith. Their health, their wealth, their happiness, their future life depended on it. No other form of propitiation of the nats, or spirits, could take its place. … The Nagas did however undertake not to sacrifice Kalas (Indians) or Kachins and to look for their victims in their own territory. (Butler 1925a, p. 4)

Because of the attitude of the Naga chiefs, Governor Butler took a cautious approach towards the abolition of human sacrifice. He explained: “… complete abolition of human sacrifice could be obtained only by an expedition and the occupation of the country …” Because of the costs and the difficulty of administering the Naga Hills, he proposed instead “that the abolition of the practice should be sought rather by cutting off the supply of victims, and through the civilizing influence of improved communications” (Burma 1925, p. 23). He did, however, warn the Naga chiefs, “The British Government cannot tolerate human sacrifice in any form and will take all measures necessary to enforce its will.” (Butler 1925b.)

These three images form a continuous excerpt from a historical account of the 1925 negotiations.

The last recorded human-sacrifice in the Naga Hills is from January 1929, however WWII may have caused a revival of the practice which would push the last sacrifices to 1946:

End of slavery

After some consultations, much exhortations, and some implied threats on the part of the Governor, the Shan and Kachin chiefs grudgingly accepted the following principles offered by the Governor. Slave-owners would be paid a cash price to be set by a British officer for each class of slave, after which the slave would be freed. The cost would then be recovered from the slave by the government in easy instalments. Emancipation would be carried out quickly. In the mean time, there was to be no trading in slaves and families of slaves were not to be broken up. After liberation, a slave would receive land and would be forever free (Butler 1925a, pp. 2–3; 1925b).

After consultations with his frontier officers, Governor Harcourt Butler of Burma endorsed a plan to liberate the slaves of Upper Burma. As a first stage, all the chiefs of the Hukawng Valley were invited to a traditional ceremonial Manao conference-feast at Maingkhwan, to be hosted by Governor Butler. A small honorarium was provided for those invited. A total of 120 chiefs and headmen attended the Manao which was held from 25 to 27 January 1925. At the Manao negotiations were held with the chiefs on the elimination of slavery and human sacrifice. Governor Butler found that the assembled chiefs were reluctantly willing to be compensated for their slaves, but chiefs of sacrificing villages were unwilling to abandon human sacrifice.
