## N American success
The camels are a North American success story. They are the basalmost of the extant artiodactyls. In their heyday, there were dozens of camel species in North America spanning a remarkable diversity of ecologies: gazelle-like stenomylines (Stenomylus), giraffe-necked browser aepycamelines (Aepycamelus), massive megatylopines (Megatylopus, Titanotylopus) and the direct ancestors of modern camels (Paracamelus) and llamas (Hemiauchenia). 

They only recently migrated to Eurasia and South America, and were made extinct in North America by Homo sapiens. Thus, they came to an end in their land of origin. 

A recent monograph of the vast fossil record of North American camels has elucidated their deep evolutionary history. The earliest whippos – the Raoellids and whales – appear around 53 Mya. In contrast, the earliest camels known to date are from 46 Mya. However, the camels branched off well before whippos; hence, we have a long prehistory of camels that remains unknown to us. We believe that, just like the Raoellids are morphologically so distinct from the whales, the earliest camels were also very distinct; therefore, we are simply failing to recognize them in the fossil record.
