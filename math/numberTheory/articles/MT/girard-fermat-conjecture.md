There is a remarkable theorem of Albert Girard that states that any prime number of the form p = 4*k+1 (where k is some integer) is the sum of two integer squares: p=a^2+b^2. Fermat later stated the same theorem to Mersenne on Christmas of 1640; hence, some people call it the Christmas theorem. However, neither of them provided a proof, which was later provided by Leonhard Euler.
5 =1^2+2^2
13 = 3^2+4^2
17 = 1^2+4^2
29 = 2^2+5^2
Mathematicians say that this simple-looking result has profound ramifications.
Given that p= a^2 + b^2; if we plot (a, b), we get a 45-degree sector between 45 and 90 degrees. Overall, the distribution of points in this sector is uniform as p goes to $\infty$. The primes of the form p=1^2+b^2 as b increases will have an angle closer to 90 degrees. Primes of the form p=a^2+b^2 where
$a
\approx b$ will have an angle of 45 degrees.

A corollary to this theorem is that in complex number space (Gaussian integers: e.g., a+ib) these are not primes but can be factored as (a+ib)*(a-ib). Those factors will lie on the points forming the 45-degree sector and its reflection along the real axis. What is "atomic" in the real space can be a "molecule" in the complex space.

While primes of the form p = 4*k+1 are always the sum of two squares, those of form p =4*k+3 are never the sum of two squares. Chebyshev made the curious observation that, typically, there are slightly more primes of the form 4*k+3 than 4*k+1 as shown in the second figure.
