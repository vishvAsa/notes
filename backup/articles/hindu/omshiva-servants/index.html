A nonprofit in Bengaluru is building India’s largest digital public library

This effort by Servants of Knowledge aims to democratise knowledge by making historical, cultural, and educational documents freely available online.
Published : Dec 16, 2024 17:38 IST - 8 MINS READ

Vikhar Ahmed Sayeed

Books on Gandhi being scanned at the Gandhi Bhavan, Bengaluru, on September 30, 2024. Books on Gandhi being scanned at the Gandhi Bhavan, Bengaluru, on September 30, 2024. | Photo Credit: K. BHAGYA PRAKASH

When you enter a large, airy room on the third floor of the salubrious you will see a group of around 15 youth diligently scanning books. The young men and women work swiftly and efficiently on ingenious scanners supervised by Omshivaprakash H. L., managing director of the Servants of Knowledge, as part of a cumulative effort to build a vast online resource known as the Public Library of India. New material will be continuously added to this repository.

So optimised are the scanning operations now, that Omshivaprakash says, “5,000 to 10,000 pages can be scanned by each scanner a day; you can hand a fat tome to us and we’ll be done in an hour.”

The Servants of Knowledge began its activities in 2015 when American technologist Carl Malamud and Omshivaprakash, an engineer, both of whom were working separately on digitising a variety of Indian materials, combined their efforts. As one of their early endeavours, they made a copy of the government of India’s digital library. That move was fortuitous as the government server crashed a couple of years later and this valuable collection went offline but continued to remain available through the access provided by the Servants of Knowledge.

The organisation has taken great strides since then: beginning with one scanner in 2018, when they began to digitise the collection of the Indian Academy of Sciences in Bengaluru, the organisation now has 18 scanners and, over the past six years has scanned collections of the Vishwakonkani Centre in Mangaluru, the Roja Muthiah Research Library in Chennai, the National Law School of India University, Azim Premji University (both in Bengaluru), the Kannada University in Hampi (a repository of all Kannada published books). The Servants of Knowledge is currently scanning several other collections, including 7,000 books at the Lalbagh Botanical Gardens in Bengaluru; it is also working with SOCHARA, a community health organisation to digitise its pamphlets.

After the pandemic, the Servants of Knowledge moved their scanners to the Gandhi Bhavan where they remain housed. A range of eclectic material is scanned here and fed into the Jai Gyan: Bharat Ek Khoj collection available on the website of the Internet Archive. As of December 2024, the Jai Gyan resource included more than 1.365 million items: books, audio and video files, magazines, and other assorted material.

The organisation, which relied mainly on volunteers all these years, was incorporated as a non-profit corporation in early 2024 with its official name being “Anubhava Mantapa Servants of Knowledge Association.” Even though the prefix “Anubhava Mantapa” is not used often when the Servants of Knowledge is mentioned, the choice clearly reveals the inspiration for the founders: Anubhava Mantapa, the first Parliament of India, founded by Basava in the 12th century as part of the social reformer’s intention to transcend the norms of caste and gender while promoting access to knowledge for all.

A bottom-up public library

This fits into the mandate of the Servants of Knowledge. Malamud explained: “The aim of the Servants of Knowledge is to build a public library of India, not a top-down big government programme but a bottom-up public library where everybody scans and shares material. So, we build this library together.”

As one component of this massive and ambitious initiative, the Servants of Knowledge digitised the substantial collection of the Gandhi Bhavan, which they subsequently presented to various Gandhi organisations in the country in August. “There were more than 11,800 books spread across three libraries in this building, with copious amounts of literature related to Gandhi, as well as Indian literature on culture, history, the freedom movement and so on,” explained Omshivaprakash.

A significant part of this collection, such as the Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi, and material published by the Navajivan Trust, is open access; but other copyrighted material can be accessed by researchers at any Gandhi organisations across the country.

The Servants of Knowledge has, however, overcome several challenges. One technical challenge arose during the COVID-19 years when it became difficult to import scanners. “That is when we realised that we should build these scanners ourselves in India,” said Omshivaprakash. The first three scanners with which the organisation started its work were based on an open-source design developed by the non-profit Internet Archive, which enthusiastically supported the Servants of Knowledge. “We were able to reverse engineer the scanners that we got and then improved them further,” explained Omshivaprakash.

Another challenge was to ensure that Indian language texts in the huge database of Jai Gyan were searchable. The technical expertise of Sushant Sinha, a computer scientist and founder of the popular legal database Indiankanoon.org., was of crucial help. “When I began to work with Carl eight years ago, one of the projects we wanted to work on was digitising Indian gazetteers. The plan was to upload them on archive.org; but back then, the Internet Archive did not support any script other than Latin,” said Sinha.

“The Optical Character Recognition (OCR) software of the Internet Archive did not recognise Indian languages so we used Google’s OCR software and reverse-engineered the way the archive worked to ensure that Indian language material could also be processed by Internet Archive,” added Sinha, who is one of the directors of the Servants of Knowledge Association and is now involved in building a searchable catalogue for the entire collection available on Jai Gyan, “which has the potential to change the knowledge landscape in India.”

Challenges over copyright

The contribution of Lawrence Liang, dean of the School of Legal and Socio-Political Studies at Dr B. R. Ambedkar University in Delhi, is crucial to copyright issues the organisation must navigate. The Servants of Knowledge has been digitising material that is both out of copyright as well as protected by copyright, which raises legal questions. “We are careful that our work conforms to Indian copyright law. Our deal with the material that we scan is simple: If any work is out of copyright and in the public domain, we post it online. If they are within copyright, we make them available for the print-disabled now,” said Malamud.

Liang explained this in greater detail: “Given the nature of copyright law in India, I think it is important to understand the way one thinks about copyright questions as absolutely settled; a new book cannot be distributed because it is copyrighted and online pirate websites that distribute these books are breaking the law. The ambiguity arises in the case of a library that has a physical copy of the book and can lend the book. There are exceptions under Section 52 of the Copyright Act, 1957, under which material can be made available for educational purposes (as per the judgment in the DU photocopy case) and for the visually impaired in a format that can only be accessed by the blind.”

The Servants of Knowledge takes a conservative approach, said Liang. There are however exceptions “that in a way reimagine the possibility of what a library can be in the 21st century and our motivation in the long run is to harness the possibilities of digitisation towards greater access.”

Malamud added, “I emphatically state that authors need to make money and so we do not take all books and put them online. We are not pirates! I was a professional writer so I get that the private publishing industry has an important role to play in the creation of knowledge, but a lot of resources such as the publications of the Indian government should be available universally.”

The Indian Copyright Act was drafted and enacted for the specific purpose of realising the fundamental aims of the Constitution of India, he said. “The increase and diffusion of knowledge is fundamental to the democratic system and that is what our effort is focussed on.”

“I would like to see us scanning one crore pages every month. We are strong in the South now although we also scan materials from all over India. But I would like to see this effort go national as a grassroots effort.”Carl MalamudAmerican technologist

The Servants of Knowledge currently scans 10 to 15 lakh pages every month but this is not enough for Malamud, the frenetic knowledge crusader, who was described as “The Internet’s Own Instigator” by Wired, the American technology magazine. “I would like to see us scanning one crore pages every month. We are strong in the South now although we also scan materials from all over India. But I would like to see this effort go national as a grassroots effort.”

Eventually, Malamud wants to be able to sell scanners to libraries, train their people to scan and serve up books “and do what we are doing, which is to accumulate something like a Gandhi library and make it available to other Gandhi libraries”.

Gandhi figures often in any conversation with Malamud and he has come up with a catchphrase drawing from the leader’s revolutionary activities during the Indian freedom struggle: “Scanning is the New Spinning.” This works appropriately as a motto for the mammoth digitisation endeavour of the Servants of Knowledge. “Everybody should be scanning materials and making them available online and sharing knowledge,” said Malamud.
